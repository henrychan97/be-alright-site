Dyslexia

Is It Dyslexia? Signs, Testing and What Parents Can Do Now

By the Be Alright Tutoring team · October 27, 2026 · 4 min read

If your child is working hard and reading still isn't clicking, the word "dyslexia" has probably crossed your mind. It's one of the most common learning differences, and it's also one of the most misunderstood. This guide covers what dyslexia actually is, the signs to watch for, how testing works, and what you can do to help your child right now, whether or not they have a diagnosis.

A quick note: this article is for general education. Only a qualified professional, such as a licensed psychologist or school evaluation team, can diagnose dyslexia.

What dyslexia is (and isn't)

Dyslexia is a specific learning difference that mainly affects word-level reading. Kids with dyslexia typically have trouble reading words accurately and fluently, and often struggle with spelling and decoding. For most, the root is difficulty with the sound structure of language: noticing, remembering and working with the individual sounds in words.

Just as important is what dyslexia is not:

  • It's not about intelligence. Many kids with dyslexia are bright, creative and highly verbal.
  • It's not "seeing letters backwards." Lots of young children reverse letters like b and d while they're learning. That alone isn't a sign of dyslexia.
  • It's not a vision problem, and it isn't caused by lack of effort or by parenting.
  • It's not something kids simply outgrow, but with the right instruction, kids with dyslexia can and do become capable readers.

Estimates of how common it is vary depending on how dyslexia is defined, but it's widely considered the most common language-based learning disability, and many more people show some of its symptoms.

Common signs by age

Preschool and kindergarten

  • Trouble learning letter names and sounds, even with lots of practice
  • Difficulty with rhyming or clapping out syllables
  • Family history of reading difficulties
  • Mixing up sounds in words or struggling to learn sequences like the alphabet

Early elementary

  • Can't sound out simple new words, or guesses based on the first letter
  • Reads slowly and with effort, even on familiar text
  • Spelling is very inconsistent, and the same word is spelled several ways
  • Understands stories read aloud much better than what they read alone

Upper elementary

  • Avoids reading aloud, and homework takes far longer than expected
  • Struggles with long, unfamiliar words
  • Strong spoken vocabulary but weak written work
  • Growing frustration or low confidence about school

Most kids show one or two of these now and then. The pattern to pay attention to is several signs that persist over time, even with good teaching and practice.

How dyslexia testing works

A dyslexia evaluation is usually done by a school psychologist, a licensed educational psychologist or a neuropsychologist. It typically looks at:

  • Phonological awareness (working with sounds in words)
  • Decoding real and made-up words
  • Rapid naming (how quickly a child names familiar things like letters or colors)
  • Reading fluency and comprehension
  • Spelling and written expression
  • Oral language and general thinking skills

Free evaluations through your public school

In the U.S., the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) gives parents the right to request an evaluation from their public school district at no cost. Put your request in writing, date it, and keep a copy. The school must respond within timelines set by your state. If your child qualifies, they may receive an IEP (Individualized Education Program) or a 504 plan with supports and accommodations.

Private evaluations

Some families choose a private evaluation for a faster timeline or a more detailed report. These can be costly, so ask your insurance provider and the evaluator what's covered before you book.

What helps kids with dyslexia

The approach with the strongest research behind it is often called structured literacy: instruction that is explicit, systematic and cumulative. In plain terms, that means:

  • Teaching letter-sound patterns directly, in a logical order
  • Lots of practice blending and breaking apart sounds
  • Reading decodable texts that match the patterns already taught
  • Frequent review so skills become automatic
  • Building vocabulary and comprehension alongside decoding

Accommodations matter too. Audiobooks, extra time on tests and having questions read aloud help kids access grade-level learning while their reading skills grow.

You don't need a label to start helping

Evaluations can take weeks or months. In the meantime, targeted reading help doesn't have to wait. The same kind of instruction that helps kids with dyslexia, clear and systematic phonics with plenty of practice, helps nearly every struggling reader. Common questions

Can a tutor diagnose dyslexia?
No. A diagnosis should come from a qualified professional, such as a licensed psychologist or a school evaluation team. A reading specialist can, however, identify which reading skills are weak and start targeted instruction right away.
Is reversing letters like b and d a sign of dyslexia?
Not on its own. Letter reversals are very common in children up to about age 7 while they're learning to read and write. Persistent trouble connecting sounds to letters is a more meaningful sign.
Can a child outgrow dyslexia?
Dyslexia is lifelong, but it doesn't define what a child can achieve. With explicit, systematic reading instruction and the right supports, many kids with dyslexia become capable, confident readers.
At what age can dyslexia be identified?
Warning signs can show up in preschool and kindergarten, and many evaluators can identify reading difficulties consistent with dyslexia by kindergarten or first grade. It's never too late to evaluate or to start helping.
