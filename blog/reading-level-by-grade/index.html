Reading levels

Reading Levels by Grade: How to Tell If Your Child Is Behind

By the Be Alright Tutoring team · October 27, 2026 · 4 min read

"She's reading at a level F." "He's below benchmark." "Her Lexile is 450." If school reports leave you with more questions than answers, you're not alone. This guide explains what kids typically can do at each grade from kindergarten through fifth, the fluency numbers many schools use, and how to tell whether your child is on track or needs more support.

First: there's more than one "reading level"

Schools use different systems, and they don't always line up neatly:

  • Guided reading levels (A to Z): letters used by systems like Fountas & Pinnell to match kids with books.
  • Lexile measures: numbers that describe both a reader's ability and a book's difficulty.
  • Benchmark screeners: short tests (for example DIBELS or similar tools) that check skills like letter sounds, decoding and fluency a few times a year.
  • Grade equivalents: a score like "2.5" meaning the child performed like a typical student halfway through 2nd grade.

A single level is a snapshot, not a verdict. What matters most is the trend over time and which specific skills are strong or weak.

What kids typically can do at each grade

Every child develops differently, so think of these as general milestones by the end of each school year, not hard cutoffs.

Kindergarten

  • Knows most letter names and the sounds they make
  • Hears and makes rhymes, and can break short words into sounds
  • Reads simple three-letter words like "cat" and "sit"
  • Recognizes a handful of common words like "the," "and," "is"

First grade

  • Sounds out one-syllable words, including blends like "st" and digraphs like "sh" and "ch"
  • Reads simple books with growing smoothness
  • Retells the main events of a story

Second grade

  • Reads two-syllable words and common vowel patterns like "ai," "ee" and "oa"
  • Reads with expression and self-corrects when something doesn't make sense
  • Answers who, what, where, when and why questions about a text

Third grade

  • Decodes longer, multisyllable words
  • Reads early chapter books independently
  • Starts using reading to learn new information in other subjects

Fourth and fifth grade

  • Reads fluently enough that most energy goes into understanding
  • Handles academic vocabulary in science and social studies texts
  • Makes inferences, compares ideas and summarizes nonfiction

Reading fluency benchmarks by grade

A quick fluency check you can try at home

  1. Pick a short passage from a book at your child's grade level that they haven't read before.
  2. Set a timer for one minute and have them read aloud.
  3. Count the words read, then subtract the words they missed or skipped.
  4. Compare that number to the table, and ask them to tell you what the passage was about.

This isn't a diagnosis, and kids have off days. But if your child is far below the middle range, reads with lots of errors, or can't tell you what they just read, it's a useful sign that a closer look is worthwhile.

Common guided reading level expectations

If your school uses letter levels, many follow a rough end-of-year pattern like this: kindergarten around D, first grade around J, second grade around M, third grade around P, fourth grade around S, and fifth grade around V. Schools set their own targets, so ask your child's teacher which level they're aiming for this year.

What "behind" really means

"Behind" can mean very different things. A child who reads accurately but slowly needs different help than a child who guesses at words or one who reads smoothly but doesn't understand. Look closely at how your child reads, not just their level. Our guide to the signs of reading struggles by age can help you name what you're seeing.

What to do if your child is behind

  1. Ask for the details. Request your child's screening scores and ask which specific skills were low.
  2. Find the root skill. Fluency or comprehension problems often come from earlier gaps in phonics or sound awareness.
  3. Practice at their true level. Use books they can read with about 95% accuracy for practice, and keep reading harder books aloud to them.
  4. Track progress over time. Recheck every few months so you know whether the plan is working.

Common questions

What reading level should a 2nd grader be at?
By the end of second grade, many kids read two-syllable words and common vowel patterns, read with expression, and answer questions about what they read. Many schools aim for about guided reading level M and roughly 100 words correct per minute by spring, but targets vary by school.
What reading level should a 3rd grader be at?
By the end of third grade, many kids read longer multisyllable words, read early chapter books on their own and use reading to learn. Common targets are around guided reading level P and roughly 112 words correct per minute in the spring.
How many words per minute should a child read?
It depends on the grade and time of year. Middle-of-the-pack spring scores in widely used norms are about 60 words correct per minute in 1st grade, 100 in 2nd, 112 in 3rd, 133 in 4th and 146 in 5th.
Is a reading level the same as a grade level?
No. Letter levels and Lexile numbers describe the books a child can read, while grade level describes where they are in school. Schools map levels to grade expectations, but those maps vary.
