How to Help a Struggling Reader at Home: 10 Simple Activities

By the Be Alright Tutoring team · October 27, 2026 · 4 min read

When your child is struggling to read, it's natural to want to do something at home. The good news is that you don't need a teaching degree or expensive materials. A few minutes a day of the right kind of practice can build skills and, just as important, confidence. Here are ten simple activities, the ground rules that make them work, and a few common habits to skip.

First, a few ground rules

  • Short and often beats long and rare. Ten to fifteen minutes a day works better than an hour on Sunday.
  • End on a win. Stop while your child still feels successful, even if that means stopping early.
  • Practice at their level, not their grade. If a book is a struggle on every line, it's too hard for practice right now.
  • Praise the effort. "You kept going on that tricky word" builds more confidence than "You're so smart."
  • Keep it warm. If reading time keeps ending in tears, change the activity. The relationship matters more than finishing the page.

Activities that build sound skills

1. Sound games in the car

Play "I spy" with sounds instead of colors: "I spy something that starts with mmm." Or start a rhyme chain: cat, hat, bat, sat. These games build phonemic awareness, the ability to hear the sounds inside words, which is the foundation for sounding words out. No paper needed.

2. Tap and blend

Say a short word like "map." Have your child tap one finger for each sound (m / a / p), then sweep their hand across and say the whole word. Then flip it: you say the sounds and they blend them together. This mirrors exactly what their brain needs to do when reading a new word.

3. Change one letter

Using magnetic letters or sticky notes, make a short word like cat. Change one letter to make hat, then hit, then hip. Kids love the puzzle, and it trains them to look at every letter in a word instead of guessing from the first one.

Activities that build fluency

4. Read it twice (or three times)

Pick a short passage at your child's level. Read it once together, then let them read it again on their own. Repeated reading is one of the best-studied ways to build fluency, because each pass gets smoother and your child hears themselves succeed.

5. Echo reading

You read one sentence with expression, and your child "echoes" it back while tracking the words with a finger. This shows them what fluent reading sounds like, including where to pause and how a question sounds.

6. Teach tricky words before the story

Before reading, look through the book for two or three words that might stop your child, like "said" or "because." Go over them together first, pointing out the parts that follow the usual sound rules and the part that's "tricky." Then the story flows better and frustration drops.

Activities that build understanding

7. Ask questions before, during and after

Before: "What do you think this book is about?" During: "Why do you think she did that?" After: "Can you tell me what happened, in order?" These quick questions teach kids that reading is about meaning, not just saying words.

8. Keep reading aloud to them

Even after your child can read on their own, keep reading books to them that are above their level. It grows vocabulary and background knowledge, and it keeps the love of stories alive while their own skills catch up.

9. Pair audiobooks with the printed book

Listening while following along in the text lets your child enjoy books their friends are reading while still seeing the words. It's a great tool alongside skill practice, not a replacement for it.

10. Celebrate progress they can see

Keep a simple chart of "words I can read now" or books finished. Seeing the list grow is powerful for kids who've started to believe they're "bad at reading."

What to avoid

  • "Look at the picture and guess." It feels helpful, but it teaches kids to skip the letters. Instead, try "Let's sound it out together."
  • Correcting every slip mid-sentence. Let small errors that don't change the meaning go, and come back to one or two words at the end.
  • Comparing siblings or classmates. Every child's path is different, and comparisons tend to shrink confidence.
  • Using reading as a punishment. Reading should feel like a treat or a cozy routine, never a consequence.

When home practice isn't enough

Home practice helps every child. But if you've been consistent for a few months and your child still isn't moving forward, or reading time keeps ending in tears, it usually means a specific skill gap needs a more targeted plan. Common questions

How long should we practice reading at home each day?
For most kids ages 4 to 11, about 10 to 20 minutes a day is plenty. Short, consistent practice that ends on a success builds more skill and confidence than long sessions.
Should I correct my child's mistakes when they read aloud?
Not every one. Let small slips that don't change the meaning go, and gently revisit one or two important words after the page. If a mistake changes the meaning, pause and say, "Does that make sense? Let's look at that word again."
Should my child read to me, or should I read to them?
Both. Your child reading aloud builds decoding and fluency, while you reading to them builds vocabulary, background knowledge and a love of stories.
My child refuses to read. What can I do?
Lower the pressure first. Try shorter sessions, easier books, sound games or audiobooks with the printed text. Strong refusal is often a sign that reading feels too hard, so it may also be worth getting a clear picture of which skill is missing.
