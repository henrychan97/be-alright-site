Choosing help

Does Online Reading Tutoring Work? What Parents Should Know

By the Be Alright Tutoring team · October 27, 2026 · 3 min read

A few years ago, most parents would never have considered online tutoring for a young child learning to read. Today it's one of the most common ways families get reading help. But does it actually work for kids ages 4 to 11? The honest answer: it depends a lot on how it's done. Here's what the research says, what separates strong online reading help from weak, and the questions to ask before you choose.

What research says about one-on-one tutoring

Tutoring is one of the best-studied ways to help kids who are behind. A large review of randomized studies by researchers Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan found that tutoring produced meaningful gains in learning, with especially strong effects for reading in the early grades and when tutors were trained teachers or professionals. Programs that met several times a week tended to do better than occasional sessions.

In other words, the ingredients that matter most are who is teaching, how often, and how personal the instruction is. Being online doesn't change those ingredients; it just changes where the lesson happens.

Why online can work especially well for young readers

  • Kids are calmer at home. No car ride, no waiting room, no classmates watching. Many anxious readers relax and take more risks.
  • Access to a true specialist. Your child can work with a trained reading specialist even if there isn't one nearby.
  • Easier consistency. Without a commute, it's simpler to keep two or more sessions a week on the calendar, which is where steady progress comes from.
  • Engaging tools. Interactive whiteboards, digital letter tiles and games keep short attention spans on track.
  • Parents can see the work. You can be nearby and pick up strategies to use between sessions.

When online reading tutoring works best

Look for these features:

  1. Live and one-on-one. A real person teaching your child in real time, not an app or a recorded video.
  2. A trained reading specialist. Teaching reading is a specific skill. A general homework helper isn't the same.
  3. It starts with an assessment. The plan should be built on exactly where your child's reading breaks down.
  4. Systematic, explicit instruction. Clear teaching of sounds, phonics, fluency and comprehension in a logical order. Our article on phonics and the science of reading explains why that matters.
  5. Enough frequency. Two or more sessions a week usually works better than one.
  6. Progress you can see. Regular reassessment and updates, so you know whether it's working.

Red flags to watch for

  • The "tutoring" is mostly an app your child uses alone
  • No initial assessment, or the same worksheets for every child
  • Tutors without specific training in teaching reading
  • No clear way to measure progress
  • Pressure to sign up before you understand the plan

Questions to ask before you choose a reading tutor

  1. What training does the tutor have in teaching reading?
  2. How do you figure out where my child is struggling?
  3. What approach do you use, and is it systematic?
  4. Will my child work with the same tutor each time?
  5. How often are sessions, and how long is each one?
  6. How do you measure progress, and how often will I hear about it?
  7. How do you keep young kids engaged online?
  8. Can you work alongside my child's school, teacher or IEP team?

Is my child too young for online tutoring?

Not usually. Even 4- and 5-year-olds do well online when sessions are interactive and paced for their age, with lots of movement, games and quick wins. A parent nearby for the first few sessions helps young kids settle in.

Setting your child up for success at home

  • A quiet spot with good lighting and few distractions
  • A laptop or tablet with a working camera, plus headphones if the house is busy
  • A consistent time slot so sessions become routine
  • A snack and a bathroom break beforehand

Common questions

Is online reading tutoring as good as in-person?
It can be, when the key ingredients are there: a live, trained reading specialist, one-on-one attention, a plan built on an assessment, and sessions at least a couple of times a week. Many kids actually focus better at home.
How often should my child have reading tutoring?
For kids who are behind, two or more sessions a week is common and tends to work better than once a week, because reading skills build through frequent, consistent practice.
How long does it take to see progress with a reading tutor?
Every child is different. Confidence often improves within the first few weeks, while measurable reading gains usually take a few months of consistent work. A good program rechecks progress regularly so you can see the change.
Can a 5-year-old do online tutoring?
Yes, when sessions are short, interactive and designed for young learners. Games, movement and quick wins keep them engaged, and a parent nearby helps at first.
