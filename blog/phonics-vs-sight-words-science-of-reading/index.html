Science of reading

Phonics vs. Sight Words: The Science of Reading, Explained for Parents

By the Be Alright Tutoring team · October 27, 2026 · 4 min read

If you've been reading about how kids learn to read, you've probably run into the phrase "science of reading," along with debates about phonics, sight words and whether children should guess words from pictures. It can feel like a tug-of-war. Here's what the research actually says, in plain English, and what it means for your child.

What "the science of reading" means

The science of reading isn't a program or a product. It's the large body of research, from education, psychology and brain science, on how people learn to read. One of its clearest findings: reading is not natural the way talking is. Kids don't pick it up just by being around books. Their brains have to be taught to connect spoken sounds to written letters.

In 2000, the National Reading Panel reviewed the research and identified five key parts of early reading instruction:

  1. Phonemic awareness: hearing and playing with the individual sounds in spoken words
  2. Phonics: connecting those sounds to letters and letter patterns
  3. Fluency: reading accurately, smoothly and with expression
  4. Vocabulary: knowing what words mean
  5. Comprehension: understanding what is read

The simple formula behind reading

Researchers often describe reading with a simple idea called the Simple View of Reading: reading comprehension = decoding × language comprehension. A child needs both: the ability to turn print into words, and the ability to understand those words. Because the two are multiplied, if either one is near zero, understanding drops. A child with a great vocabulary still can't understand a book they can't decode, and a child who decodes perfectly still needs words and background knowledge to make sense of it.

Phonics: the "how" of reading new words

Phonics teaches kids the code: which letters and letter combinations stand for which sounds. Systematic phonics teaches these patterns in a planned order, from simple (like m, s, a) to complex (like "igh" or "tion"), with plenty of practice reading and spelling words that use them. The National Reading Panel found that systematic phonics instruction helps children learn to read, and is especially helpful for kids who are struggling.

So what about sight words?

"Sight words" usually means common words kids should recognize instantly, like "the," "was," "said" and "because." Recognizing these quickly is important. The question is how kids get there.

Research on how the brain stores words (often called orthographic mapping) shows that kids don't memorize words as pictures. They store them by connecting each letter to its sound. That's why many teachers now teach common words with sounds too: most of the letters in "said" follow the rules (s and d), and only one part is "tricky" and needs to be learned "by heart." Some teachers call these heart words.

So it isn't really phonics versus sight words. Strong phonics is what turns words into "sight words," words a child reads instantly and effortlessly.

Why guessing from pictures can backfire

Some older approaches teach kids to figure out unknown words using the picture, the first letter, or what "makes sense." It can look like reading in simple picture books, but it teaches a habit of guessing instead of looking at all the letters. When kids reach books with fewer pictures and harder words, often around second or third grade, that habit stops working, and kids who seemed fine can suddenly look like they're falling behind.

Instead of "What would make sense?" try "Let's look at all the letters and sound it out." Then check: "Does that make sense?"

Questions to ask your child's school

  • Do you teach phonics systematically, in a set order?
  • Do beginning readers practice with decodable books that match the phonics patterns they've learned?
  • How do you teach common irregular words?
  • How do you screen for reading difficulties, and how often?
  • What extra help is available if my child is behind?

How to put this into practice at home

  • Play sound games: rhyming, "what's the first sound?" and blending sounds into words
  • When your child gets stuck, point to the letters and sound it out together
  • Practice with books that use the patterns your child knows
  • Keep reading aloud to them to grow vocabulary and background knowledge

Is it too late for phonics for an older child?

No. Many 9-, 10- and 11-year-olds who struggle with reading have gaps in phonics that were never fully filled. Going back to fill those gaps, in a way that respects their age and doesn't feel babyish, can make a real difference in how they read longer words and how confident they feel.

Common questions

Is memorizing sight words bad?
Recognizing common words quickly is important. The research suggests kids learn them best by connecting the letters to sounds rather than memorizing whole-word shapes, so many teachers now teach high-frequency words with phonics too.
What is the science of reading in simple terms?
It's the large body of research on how children learn to read. Its main message is that reading must be taught explicitly, including how letters connect to sounds, along with fluency, vocabulary and comprehension.
What is three-cueing and why is it criticized?
Three-cueing encourages kids to figure out words using pictures, context and the first letter. Critics point out that it teaches guessing instead of decoding, which can break down as books get harder.
Is it too late to teach phonics to a 10-year-old?
No. Older struggling readers often have phonics gaps, and filling them with age-appropriate, systematic instruction can improve both accuracy and confidence.
